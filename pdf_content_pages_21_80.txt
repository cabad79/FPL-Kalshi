CHAPTER 1
The Soccer Analytics Landscape
It is Saturday evening. You are at home watching Liverpool play. Before kickoff, you
placed a small, casual bet on them to win—not as a professional strategy, but simply
to make the match more engaging. As the game unfolds, your phone rests nearby
with a live betting app open. The win probability updates continuously.
Liverpool strings together several passes in the opponent’s half. The probability shifts
from 54% to 57%. A sudden counterattack follows. A dangerous shot. The probability
drops to 49%. The score has not changed. Yet your confidence moves with the
numbers.
This experience captures a defining feature of modern soccer: the game is no longer
interpreted solely through narrative and intuition. Increasingly, it is accompanied—
and often understood—through probabilities.
But when a model assigns a 49% chance of victory, several natural questions imme‐
diately arise. What determines those probabilities? What assumptions underlie that
number? How stable are these estimates? How should we interpret them? Which
signals in the data are meaningful, and which are merely noise?
Answering these questions requires understanding the unique analytical challenges of
soccer. The sport is low scoring and highly variable. Outcomes often hinge on a small
number of events. Apparent dominance may not translate into goals, while short-
term performance swings may reflect randomness rather than structural change.
Human perception gravitates toward memorable moments; statistical reasoning, in
contrast, requires consistency across many moments.
Soccer analytics exists within this tension. This chapter provides an overview of the
modern soccer analytics landscape. We discuss the types of questions soccer analytics
seeks to answer, the kinds of data available, the perspectives through which analysis
can be conducted, the modeling approaches commonly used, and the conceptual

The Problems That Soccer Analytics Helps Solve
Soccer analytics is not pursued simply for intellectual curiosity. In practice, it exists to
support decisions.
Across professional clubs, leagues, media organizations, and betting markets, data-
driven analysis helps answer a wide range of operational and strategic questions.
The resulting decisions vary in scale—from real-time tactical adjustments during a
match to long-term roster construction and financial planning. Understanding these
decision contexts clarifies why analytics has become an integral part of modern
soccer organizations.
Soccer Clubs: Making Decisions Across the Organization
Within professional soccer clubs, analytics supports a wide range of decisions across
coaching, player management, and organizational operations.
Match preparation and tactical decisions
One of the most visible applications of analytics lies in match preparation and tactical
planning. Coaching staffs must evaluate opponents, determine formations, select
starting lineups, and plan substitution strategies. These decisions often rely on a
combination of experience, video review, and statistical analysis.
Data analysts contribute by examining patterns across historical matches and iden‐
tifying measurable tendencies. For example, analysts may evaluate how a team per‐
forms under different pressing intensities, how certain formations influence shot
creation, or how substitution timing affects late-game scoring probabilities.

Betting Markets: Modeling Uncertainty and Pricing Outcomes
Soccer analytics also plays a central role in sports betting markets.
Bookmakers such as bet365, DraftKings, and other betting platforms must set odds
that reflect the probability of multiple match outcomes. These odds act as market pri‐
ces that allow betting activity to occur while also managing the financial exposure of
the bookmaker. In practice, odds must strike a careful balance: they must be accurate
enough to reflect the underlying probabilities of outcomes while also accounting for
market behavior and betting volume.

To accomplish this, bookmakers rely on statistical models that estimate probabilities
for events such as match outcomes, total goals, scorelines, and other in-game occur‐
rences. These models typically incorporate large historical datasets that capture team
performance, scoring patterns, and contextual factors. Variables may include meas‐
ures of team strength, recent form, home-field advantage, injuries, travel schedules,
and historical head-to-head matchups.
Many betting models build upon classical statistical frameworks such as Poisson
goal models, which estimate the expected number of goals scored by each team.
More advanced systems may incorporate machine learning methods that combine
hundreds of predictive features derived from event data, rankings, and market
information.
The objective of these models is not perfect prediction. Soccer remains a low-scoring
and high-variance sport, where rare events and randomness can strongly influence
outcomes. Instead, bookmakers aim to produce well-calibrated probabilities that
allow markets to function efficiently while maintaining balanced risk exposure.
In addition to internal modeling, bookmakers must also react to the behavior of
the betting market itself. When large volumes of wagers arrive on one side of a
market, odds may adjust to rebalance risk. As a result, betting markets represent an
interaction between statistical forecasting and market dynamics.
Because betting markets update continuously as new information arrives—lineups,
injuries, weather conditions, or match events—probabilistic modeling and uncer‐
tainty estimation are central to their operation. Live betting systems in particular
must update probabilities in real time as the match state evolves.
For analysts and researchers, betting markets also provide a useful benchmark.
Because odds reflect the aggregated expectations of sophisticated models and
informed bettors, they are often treated as a reference point when evaluating predic‐
tive models in soccer analytics.

The Data-Driven Evolution in Soccer
Soccer’s transformation from an intuition-driven craft to a data-informed discipline
has reshaped the way the game is understood, analyzed, and managed at every level.
This section traces that evolution—from the limits of traditional analysis, through
the data infrastructure that made new approaches possible, to the metrics and appli‐
cations that define the field today.
From Outcomes to Process: The Limits of Traditional Analysis
Soccer has long been described as the beautiful game—fluid, creative, and often
unpredictable. For decades, match analysis relied primarily on observational exper‐
tise. Coaches and analysts interpreted performance through video review, accumula‐
ted experience, and a limited set of summary statistics such as goals scored, goals
conceded, and league standings. These tools remain valuable, but they provide only a
relatively coarse and often subjective view of performance.
Traditional evaluation also depended heavily on final outcomes. A league table, for
example, awards the same three points for a 1–0 win against a title contender as for
a 1–0 win against a relegation candidate. This approach is effective for determining
standings, but it captures little about the contextual difficulty or underlying quality of
each result. This limitation is especially consequential in soccer because the sport is
relatively low scoring, and outcomes are often shaped by a small number of events—a
deflection, a refereeing decision, or a single moment of individual brilliance. As a
result, match outcomes alone can be noisy indicators of team quality.

Modern soccer analytics developed in part as a response to this problem. Instead
of focusing only on whether a team won or lost, analysts increasingly examine the
processes that generate outcomes: how chances were created, how defensive pressure
was applied, how possession moved through space, and how players contributed both
on and off the ball. These intermediate signals are often more informative than raw
results for evaluating performance and, in many settings, more useful for predicting
future outcomes.
The Data Revolution: From Event Logs to Tracking Systems
This shift has been enabled by a dramatic increase in the granularity of available
data. Over the past two decades, several developments have transformed the informa‐
tional environment of the sport. Event data records on-ball actions such as passes,
shots, tackles, dribbles, interceptions, and fouls, typically with timestamps and spatial
coordinates. This makes it possible to reconstruct sequences of play and quantify
contributions such as progressive passing, entries into dangerous areas, and posses‐
sion patterns.
Tracking data adds another layer of detail. Optical tracking systems and other elec‐
tronic performance and tracking systems record the positions of players and the
ball multiple times per second, making it possible to study off-ball movement, team
shape, spacing, pressing behavior, and spatial control in ways that were previously
difficult to measure. FIFA’s electronic performance and tracking systems framework
explicitly covers both camera-based and wearable technologies for measuring posi‐
tional and physical information.
Training and physiological data further extend the analytical scope beyond match
play. Wearable systems can help monitor workload, movement intensity, and
recovery-related signals, supporting player development, load management, and
injury-risk reduction. FIFA likewise notes that these systems may be combined with
accelerometers, gyroscopes, and heart-rate monitoring to measure load and physio‐
logical parameters.
Underpinning all this is modern computational infrastructure. Cloud computing,
large-scale data pipelines, and machine learning frameworks allow analysts to process
large match databases, train predictive models, and simulate many possible scenarios
efficiently. This has helped move soccer analysis beyond descriptive summaries and
toward probabilistic modeling and decision support.

will result in a goal based on contextual information such as shot location and chance

explanations of matches, and betting markets continuously update probabilities as
new information becomes available. At the same time, analytics does not replace
traditional expertise. Video analysis remains essential for interpreting tactical intent,
and coaching judgment provides context that numbers alone cannot fully capture.
In practice, analytics is most useful when it complements rather than substitutes for
scouting, tactical observation, and domain knowledge.
At its core, soccer analytics still addresses familiar questions: Why did a particular
outcome occur? What is likely to happen next? What decisions can improve the
chances of success? What has changed is not the curiosity itself, but the quality, scale,
and structure of the data used to investigate those questions.

Soccer is not only a complex sport but also a difficult statistical environment: events
are sparse, outcomes are highly variable, and contextual factors often influence per‐
formance in subtle ways. The next section directly examines these challenges before
introducing the analytical framework that guides the rest of the book.
Why Soccer Resists Easy Analysis
Soccer presents several characteristics that make predictive modeling particularly
challenging. First, it is a low-scoring sport. Matches often contain only a small
number of goals, which increases variance and reduces the signal-to-noise ratio. A
single event can disproportionately influence the outcome of a match. Second, soccer
is continuous and state-dependent. Events occur sequentially and interact with one
another. A defensive mistake early in the match may change tactical behavior for

other sports, substitution opportunities are restricted, and tactical adjustments occur

Fourth, context matters greatly. Opponent strength, match state, fatigue, travel sched‐
ules, and player availability all influence performance.
These contextual variables often interact in complex ways. Some influential factors

cation among players, or tactical discipline may shape match outcomes without
being fully captured in event data. These characteristics mean that soccer models
must be carefully evaluated. Predictive systems require calibrated probabilities, robust
validation procedures, and cautious interpretation. In this book, we rely on several
guiding principles while acknowledging these challenges. Together, they shape the
way models are designed, evaluated, and interpreted.
A Framework for Soccer Analysis
Soccer analytics is ultimately about improving the way we reason about a sport
that contains substantial uncertainty. Analytical methods attempt to distinguish
short-term fluctuations from the underlying signals that reflect team quality and
performance.
To make sense of the field, this section organizes soccer analytics along three com‐
plementary axes: the types of analysis that describe the kinds of questions analysts
attempt to answer, the data sources that enable modern analysis, and the levels of
aggregation at which soccer performance can be studied. Together, these dimensions
form the framework for most modern soccer analysis.

Data Sources for Modern Analysis
The growth of soccer analytics has been driven largely by the increasing richness and
resolution of available data. Several categories now form the foundation of modern
analysis. A summary is shown in Table 1-2.
Event data records discrete on-ball actions during a match. Passes, shots, tackles,
interceptions, dribbles, and fouls are logged with timestamps and spatial coordinates.
Because it captures the sequence of play, event data allows analysts to reconstruct
attacking buildup, measure ball progression, and evaluate how teams create scoring
opportunities. Event data is also the most widely available data source and forms the
primary dataset used in this book.
Tracking data provides much finer spatial resolution. Optical camera systems or GPS
sensors capture the positions of all players and the ball many times per second—
often around 25 frames per second. This data reveals the spatial context behind
events, allowing analysts to study defensive shape, spacing between players, pressing
intensity, and off-ball movement.
Physiological and performance data records metrics such as sprint counts, high-
intensity running distance, heart rate, and recovery indicators. These measurements
are typically used by sports science departments to monitor player workload, reduce
injury risk, and support long-term player development.

From Player to League: Levels of Aggregation
Soccer performance can be studied at different levels of aggregation, and the choice of
level shapes both what questions can be asked and how reliably they can be answered.
Table 1-3 provides a summary.
At the player level, analysts attempt to isolate the contribution of individual actions.
Modern metrics such as on-ball value (OBV) and valuing actions by estimating prob‐
abilities (VAEP) estimate how much a single pass, shot, or defensive action changes a
team’s probability of scoring or conceding. These approaches are particularly valuable
in scouting and recruitment.
At the team level, individual actions are aggregated into collective performance indi‐
cators. Analysts examine patterns such as shot quality, defensive efficiency, possession
structure, and chance creation. Because team-level metrics tend to be more stable
over time than individual statistics, they provide a stronger foundation for predictive
modeling.
At the match level, analysts focus on the dynamics of a single game. Contextual
factors such as opponent strength, scoreline, red cards, and game state strongly
influence team behavior. Adjusting for these conditions helps separate underlying
team quality from situational effects.
At the league level, analysis provides the broader competitive context. Teams compete
within environments that differ in playing style, pace, and scoring frequency. League-
level adjustments allow analysts to account for differences in competition quality and
strength of schedule when comparing performance across competitions.

Principles That Run Through Everything
Beyond layers, data, and levels, several conceptual ideas appear repeatedly through‐
out modern soccer analytics. Rather than isolated rules, they form a connected way of
thinking about evidence and inference in a noisy, complex sport.
The first is the distinction between association and causation. Statistical relationships
do not automatically imply causal explanations. A variable may improve the predic‐
tive accuracy of a model without representing a true causal mechanism. Certain
passing patterns may correlate with winning, for instance, yet the underlying driver
might be broader tactical structure rather than the passes themselves. Analysts must
therefore interpret predictive variables with care, resisting the temptation to read
causation into correlation.
The second is heterogeneity. Teams differ substantially in playing style, tactical philos‐
ophy, financial resources, and competitive environment. Models that assume uniform
behavior across all teams often perform poorly precisely because they ignore these
structural differences. Recognizing heterogeneity helps analysts design models that
remain robust across leagues, seasons, and tactical contexts.
The third is the importance of contextual comparison. Performance metrics are
meaningful only when interpreted relative to context. Raw totals—goals scored, pos‐
session percentage—do not automatically indicate team strength. A team that scores
frequently against weaker opposition may not perform as well against stronger com‐
petition. Ranking systems and strength-of-schedule adjustments provide a structured
way to make comparisons that are fair and informative.

Conclusion
Soccer analytics is no longer a niche pursuit; it has become a fundamental lens
through which the game is understood, managed, and enjoyed. The shift from
intuition-driven observation to data-informed analysis has reshaped decision mak‐

moving beyond raw outcomes to examine the underlying processes, analysts can now
quantify performance, evaluate risk, and uncover patterns that traditional methods
often miss. Yet the sport’s inherent low-scoring nature, continuous flow, and complex
contextual dependencies ensure that modeling soccer remains a challenging endeavor
that requires both statistical rigor and deep domain knowledge.

Understanding Soccer Data Levels
Before we dive into the data, let’s talk about how it’s organized. Think of it like
watching a game from different perspectives. You could be in a helicopter seeing the
whole stadium, on the sideline watching the teams, or right on the pitch following
a single player. Soccer data has a similar layered structure, and understanding these
levels is crucial because the kinds of questions you can answer depend on the level of
data you have.
Match-Level Data
Match-level data is the 30,000-foot view. It gives you the final score, the teams that
played, the competition name, and the date. It’s the kind of information you’d find

aggregated across many matches—for example, all games in the group stage of a
tournament. Match-level data is perfect for answering high-level questions like these:
• Who won the most games in the group stage?
•
• Which teams scored the most goals in the tournament?
•
• How many matches went to extra time?
•
You can’t analyze a team’s passing patterns with only match-level data, but you can
identify trends across competitions and seasons. It’s the foundation for tournament
analysis and league standings.

How many kilometers did Sam Kerr run? How accurate were Alex Morgan’s passes?
Player-level data is often aggregated from finer-grained event data and helps us
compare players across roles and teams. It’s essential for the following:
• Player recruitment and scouting

• Performance evaluation and ratings

• Fantasy soccer and player awards

• Assessment of individual contributions to team success

Event-Level Data
Event-level data provides the most granular view, putting you right on the pitch with
the players. It records every individual event in a match—each pass, shot, tackle,
dribble, or foul—along with who did it, where it happened on the pitch, and when.
Event data does not, however, tell you where all the other players were on the pitch at
that moment; it tracks only on-ball actions.
In soccer analytics, the term event refers specifically to any discrete on-ball action that
changes or continues possession. It doesn’t mean tournament or competition event, as
in everyday English.
Event-level data allows analysts to study these elements:
• Tactical patterns and team formations
•
• Player actions and decision making
•
• The flow and momentum of matches
•
• Spatial control and territorial dominance
•
The beauty of event-level data is that you can aggregate it upward to create player-
level or match-level summaries—but not the other way around. Once you have event
data, you can answer questions at any level of detail.
Understanding these levels is crucial because each connects to a different class of
analytical questions. You can’t determine a tournament winner from a single pass,

Handling Missing Values
Real soccer data is rarely spotless. Passes may be logged without outcomes, coordi‐
nates may be missing, or player IDs may be absent for substitutions. If we do not
carefully handle these gaps, later summaries and models can become skewed or
unstable.
The following line produces a quick summary of where in our events_df dataset data
gaps might be hiding:
events_df.isnull().sum()
This command produces a list of the number of missing values of each table column.
Fields such as pass_outcome_name or location often appear with gaps. (Spoiler alert:
StatsBomb data is, in fact, very clean. This will likely not be the case, however, for
other data that you might deal with later in your analyst career!)
What should we do with missing or messy values? The answer depends on context:
• Drop values when they are rare and nonessential.
•
• Impute (fill in) when the missing value has a natural default.
•
• Interpret carefully when the missing value itself carries information.
•
