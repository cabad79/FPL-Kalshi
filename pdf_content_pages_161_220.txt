K-Nearest Neighbors: Finding Similar Situations
While logistic regression is powerful, it makes assumptions about the relationship
between features and outcomes (specifically, that it’s linear in the log-odds space).
Sometimes you want an algorithm that makes fewer assumptions and instead learns
directly from the data. Enter KNN.
The Intuition: Birds of a Feather
KNN is beautifully simple. To predict the class of a new data point, it looks at the k
closest data points in the training set (the nearest neighbors) and takes a vote. If most
of those neighbors are goals, the model predicts a goal. If most neighbors are not, the
model predicts no goal.
Imagine you’re trying to decide whether a shot will be a goal. You look at the five
most similar shots in your historical data (similar distance, similar angle). If four out
of those five were goals, you’d probably predict this one will be a goal too. That’s KNN
in a nutshell.

The key parameter is k, the number of neighbors to consider. A small k (like 1
or 3) makes the model very sensitive to individual data points, which can lead to
overfitting. A large k (like 50 or 100) makes the model smoother but might miss
important small-scale patterns. Choosing the right k is an art and a science.
KNN commonly uses Euclidean distance, which measures the straight-line distance
between two points in multidimensional feature space. However, this calculation
becomes problematic when features have different units, such as comparing a player’s
height in centimeters (ranging from 150–200) against their goals scored in single
digits. Since Euclidean distance is sensitive to the magnitude of each feature, the
height values would dominate the distance calculation simply because they are larger
numbers. To prevent this, it is recommended to scale features (typically using stand‐
ardization or min-max normalization) so that all variables contribute equally to the
distance measurement and the algorithm can find truly meaningful neighbors.

true patterns but also simple enough to ignore random noise.

Practical Tips and Common Pitfalls
As you embark on your own classification projects, here are some hard-won lessons
to keep in mind.
Handling Imbalanced Data
In soccer, many classification problems suffer from class imbalance. For example, in
our xG model, only about 12% of shots result in goals. This means the model could
achieve 88% accuracy by simply predicting No Goal every time, which is obviously
useless.
When dealing with imbalanced data, consider these strategies:
Use appropriate metrics
Don’t rely solely on accuracy. Focus on precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC,
which give a more nuanced view of performance.
Adjust class weights
Many algorithms, including logistic regression in scikit-learn, allow you to set
class_weight='balanced', which automatically adjusts for imbalance.
Resample your data
You can either oversample the minority class (create more copies of rare events)
or undersample the majority class (remove some common events). Libraries like
imbalanced-learn provide sophisticated resampling techniques.
Change the decision threshold
Instead of using 0.5 as the cutoff for predicting the positive class, experiment
with different thresholds based on your specific needs.
Selecting and Engineering Features
The features you choose can make or break your model. Here are some tips:
Start simple
Begin with a few intuitive features. It’s easier to understand and debug a simple
model.
Use your domain knowledge
Use your understanding of soccer to create meaningful features. Distance and
angle to goal are obvious for xG, but what about the number of defenders
between the shooter and the goal?

Avoid multicollinearity
If two features are highly correlated (e.g., total passes and successful passes),
they might confuse the model. Consider removing one or combining them. More
details are provided in Chapter 6.
Normalize your features
Some algorithms (like KNN) are sensitive to the scale of features. If one feature
ranges from 0 to 100 and another from 0 to 1, the first will dominate. Use
standardization or normalization to put them on the same scale.
Avoiding Overfitting
As noted in Chapter 4, overfitting occurs when your model learns the training data
too well, including its noise and peculiarities, and fails to generalize to new data. It’s
like a student who memorizes the practice exam but can’t handle new questions.
To avoid overfitting:
Use a validation set
Always split your data into training, validation, and test sets. Train on the
training set, tune hyperparameters on the validation set, and evaluate only final
performance on the test set.
Cross-validation
Use k-fold cross-validation to get a more robust estimate of your model’s perfor‐
mance.
Regularization
Techniques like L1 and L2 regularization penalize overly complex models. More
details are given in Chapter 6.
Keep it simple
A model with fewer features is less likely to overfit. Use feature selection tech‐
niques to identify the most important predictors.

Choosing When to Use Which Algorithm
You now have two classification algorithms in your toolkit: logistic regression and
KNN. When should you use each?
Logistic regression
Use when you want interpretability, when you believe the relationship between
features and outcome is roughly linear (in log-odds space), and when you have
a moderate to large dataset. This algorithm is fast, reliable, and a great starting
point.
KNN
Use when the decision boundary is complex and nonlinear, when you have a
small to medium dataset, and when you don’t need to understand why the model
makes its predictions. Be aware that KNN can be slow with large datasets because
it needs to calculate distances to all training points.
As a general rule, start with logistic regression. If it doesn’t perform well, explore
more complex algorithms like KNN, decision trees, or neural networks (which we’ll
cover in later chapters).
Tuning the Decision Threshold: Finding the Sweet Spot
So far, we’ve been using 0.5 as the threshold for converting probabilities to class
predictions. If the model predicts a probability greater than 0.5, we classify it as a
goal; otherwise, it’s not a goal. But who says 0.5 is the right threshold? Depending on
your specific needs, you might want to adjust this threshold to optimize for different
outcomes.
Understanding the Threshold Trade-Off
Changing the threshold shifts the balance between precision and recall. A lower
threshold (say, 0.3) makes the model more aggressive: it will predict Goal more often,
increasing recall (catching more actual goals) but decreasing precision (more false
alarms). A higher threshold (say, 0.7) makes the model more conservative: it will
predict Goal only when very confident, increasing precision but decreasing recall.
Think of this trade-off like a referee deciding whether to award a penalty. A lenient
referee (low threshold) will award more penalties, catching more legitimate fouls but
also making more mistakes. A strict referee (high threshold) will award penalties only
for clear fouls, making fewer mistakes but also missing some legitimate calls.

Deciding When to Adjust the Threshold
In practice, the right threshold depends on the cost of different types of errors:
High recall priority
If missing a goal prediction is very costly (e.g., you’re trying to identify all
dangerous attacking situations for defensive analysis), use a lower threshold.
You’ll catch more goals, even if you have more false alarms.
High precision priority

to take a shot or pass, and taking a low-probability shot is wasteful), use a higher
threshold. You’ll predict goals only when very confident.
Balanced approach
If both types of errors are equally important, use the threshold that maximizes
the F1 score.

Handling Missing Data
In real-world soccer data, you’ll often encounter missing values. A player’s position
might not be recorded for every event, or a shot’s technique might be unknown. The
way you handle missing data can significantly impact your model’s performance.
Here are common strategies:
Remove rows with missing values
Simple but wasteful. Use this approach only if you have plenty of data and few
missing values.
Impute with mean/median
Replace missing values with the feature’s mean (for continuous variables) or
median (for skewed distributions).
Impute with mode
Replace missing values with the most common value (for categorical variables).
Use predictive imputation
Use a model to predict missing values based on other features. This option is
more sophisticated but can be powerful.
Create a missing indicator
Add a binary feature that flags whether a value was missing. This can help the
model learn patterns in missingness.

Real-World Considerations
Building a classification model in a Jupyter notebook is one thing; deploying it in the
real world is another. Here are some practical considerations to keep in mind.
Data Quality and Availability
Your model is only as good as your data. In professional soccer analytics, you might
have access to high-quality tracking data with precise player positions. But if you’re
working with a lower-league team or analyzing historical matches, you might be
limited to basic event data or even manually collected statistics.
Always assess your data quality before building a model. Are there missing values?
Are the measurements accurate? Is the data representative of the scenarios you
want to predict? A simple model trained on high-quality data will almost always
outperform a complex model trained on noisy, incomplete data.
Model Maintenance and Updates
Soccer is a dynamic sport. Tactics evolve, players improve or decline, and rules
change. A model trained on data from five years ago might not perform well on
today’s game. Plan for regular model updates and retraining.
Set up a monitoring system to track your model’s performance over time. If you
notice the accuracy or AUC dropping, it’s a sign that the model needs to be retrained
with more recent data.
Ethical Considerations
As you build predictive models, remember that they can have real-world con‐
sequences. A model that predicts player performance might influence transfer
decisions, affecting players’ careers and livelihoods. A model that predicts match
outcomes might be used for betting, raising ethical and legal concerns.
Always consider the potential impact of your models. Be transparent about their
limitations, avoid making claims of certainty, and ensure that your work is used
responsibly.

The Classification Tree: Soccer’s Decision Flowchart
Imagine you are a coach analyzing a missed scoring chance. Your thought process
isn’t a single calculation; it’s a flowchart of questions. Was the shooter under pressure?
Was the shot taken on their preferred foot? Was the angle too narrow? Each answer
leads to another question until you reach a conclusion: That should have been a goal.
A classification tree formalizes this exact process.
The Intuition: Learning the If-Then Rules of Soccer
A classification tree is a supervised learning algorithm that partitions the feature space
into rectangular regions, making a prediction based on the majority class in each
region. Think of it as playing 20 questions with your data. To classify a shot as a goal
or no-goal, the tree might ask these questions:
1. Is the shot distance less than 18 meters? If yes, go to question 2; if no, predict No
1.
Goal.
2. Is the shot angle greater than 20 degrees? If yes, go to question 3; if no, predict No
2.
Goal.
3. Was it a first-time shot? If yes, predict Goal; if no, predict No Goal.
3.
This sequence creates a transparent, human-readable model. The path from the top
(the root) to the bottom (a leaf) represents a decision rule.

The Intuition: Ensemble Learning
Imagine you want to predict the outcome of a match. Instead of asking one expert,
you ask a hundred. Each expert has slightly different knowledge and biases. While
some might be wrong, the collective wisdom of the crowd is likely to be more
accurate than any single expert. This is the core idea of a random forest.
It builds hundreds of decision trees, but with two key twists:
Bagging (bootstrap aggregating)
Each tree is trained on a random sample of the training data, drawn with replace‐
ment. This means some matches will appear multiple times in a tree’s training set,
while others won’t appear at all.
Feature randomness
At each split in a tree, the algorithm considers only a random subset of features.
This prevents any single strong feature (like distance_to_goal) from dominat‐
ing all the trees.
These two techniques ensure that the trees in the forest are diverse. To make a
prediction, the random forest simply takes a majority vote from all the individual
trees. This averaging process cancels out the noise and instability of individual trees,
leading to a much more robust and accurate model.

One of the clever features of random forests is the out-of-bag (OOB) error estimate.
Remember that each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the data, meaning some
data points are left out. These left-out samples can be used as a validation set for that
particular tree. By aggregating predictions across all trees for which a data point was
OOB, we get a free cross-validation estimate without needing a separate validation
set.
This is particularly useful when working with limited data, as it allows you to use
all available data for training while still getting an honest estimate of model perfor‐
mance. In scikit-learn, you can enable this by setting oob_score=True when creating
your random forest classifier.

XGBoost: Learning from Mistakes
A random forest builds an army of independent experts. XGBoost takes a different
approach: it builds a team of specialists who learn from one another’s mistakes. In
this sequential process, each new tree is built to correct the errors of the previous one.
The Intuition: Sequential Learning
Imagine you’re trying to predict expected goals (xG). Your first model is a simple one
that looks only at shot distance. It does OK, but it makes a lot of mistakes. XGBoost
then builds a second model, but instead of trying to predict xG directly, it tries to
predict the errors (the residuals) of the first model. By adding the predictions of this
second model to the first, you get a more accurate overall prediction.
XGBoost repeats this process hundreds or thousands of times. Each new tree is a
small, weak learner that focuses on the remaining errors. By chaining them together,
XGBoost creates an incredibly powerful and accurate final model. This technique,
called gradient boosting, is one of the most successful and widely used algorithms in
machine learning competitions and real-world applications.

Tuning XGBoost: The Art of Hyperparameter Optimization
XGBoost’s power comes with complexity. It has many hyperparameters that control
the learning process. These are the most important ones for soccer analytics:
learning_rate (or eta)
Controls how much each tree contributes to the final prediction. Lower values
(0.01–0.1) require more trees but often lead to better performance.
max_depth
The maximum depth of each tree. Typical values range from 3 to 10. Deeper trees
can model more complex patterns but risk overfitting.
n_estimators
The number of boosting rounds (trees). More is generally better, but with dimin‐
ishing returns and increased computation time.
subsample
The fraction of samples used for each tree. Values like 0.8 introduce randomness
and help prevent overfitting.
colsample_bytree
The fraction of features used for each tree. This is similar to random forest’s
max_features.
reg_alpha and reg_lambda
L1 and L2 regularization terms. These penalize complex models and help prevent
overfitting.
A typical tuning workflow starts with default parameters, then uses cross-validation
to systematically explore different combinations. Grid search or randomized search
can help find optimal values, but be prepared for this to take time because XGBoost
training can be computationally expensive.

Why Deep Learning for Soccer? Beyond Traditional Models
One useful way to build intuition is to imagine a team moving the ball from the
goalkeeper through the back line, midfield, and attack. The ball starts deep, moves
through different lines, and eventually reaches the final attacking action. In a neural
network, data moves in a similar way: it enters the model, passes through several
layers, and eventually produces a prediction. In this analogy, each neuron in the
network is like a player handling and passing information, while each layer is like a
unit of the team. Figure 7-1 illustrates this.
The analogy is only for intuition. Neural networks were originally inspired by simpli‐
fied ideas about the way biological neurons receive signals, process them, and pass
them on. For our purposes, what matters is the structure: information comes in, is
transformed through several layers, and leaves the network as an output.
In practice, a neural network has three main types of layers. The input layer receives
the raw features, such as soccer statistics or engineered variables. One or more
hidden layers transform those inputs into increasingly useful internal representations.
A network with two or more hidden layers is usually described as a deep learning
model. Finally, the output layer produces the prediction, such as a win probability, a
match result, or an expected goal count.
Each layer consists of individual neurons. Each neuron comes with an activation
function, which helps govern when it passes information onward and when it stays
quiet. Between these layers of neurons, forward propagation is the process that moves
information through the network. Finally, a loss function assesses the difference
between reality and the neural network’s prediction—in essence, a measure of the
model’s quality.

Before we dive into coding, let’s understand just enough about how a neural network
works to make sense of the models we’ll build. Our conceptual overview will cover

Neurons
Just like lines in a formation are made up by individual soccer players, layers in
neural networks are made up by neurons (hence the neural in neural networks). Every
neuron is connected to all the neurons in the layers before and after it, but not to the
neurons in its own layer.
The knowledge of a neural network is quantified in weights. You can think of them as
the strength of the connections between individual neurons. Some neurons might be
very connected, others less so.
For example, teams that take many shots on target typically score more goals. In
a neural network, the connection (weight) between a neuron representing shots on
target (near the input layer) and a neuron representing goals scored (in the output
layer) would become strong as the model learns this relationship.
In addition to weights, each neuron has a bias—a kind of built-in tendency that
shifts its decision making independent of the inputs. In the soccer analogy, you might

import numpy as np
def relu(x):
    return np.maximum(0, x)
def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
def forward_propagation(X, weights, biases):
    """Run forward propagation for a two-layer network."""
    # Hidden layer: Z = XW + b
    z1 = np.dot(X, weights[0]) + biases[0]
    # Apply ReLU nonlinearity
    a1 = relu(z1)
    # Output layer: Z = AW + b
    z2 = np.dot(a1, weights[1]) + biases[1]
    # Convert logits to probabilities
    a2 = sigmoid(z2)
    return a2
The input layer X is typically our match data (shots, passes, possession, etc.). The hid‐

ball travels through different lines. The output layer applies another transformation
and sigmoid. This gives us a probability, such as how likely is a home win?
In practice, weights and biases (which are inputs to the forward propagation func‐
tion) are not chosen by hand. Instead, the training sets them based on thousands
of matches. Here we’re just illustrating how information flows from raw features,
through hidden decision layers, to a prediction. If you continue the soccer analogy,
the final prediction is like the shot at the end of the move: the whole attacking

Learning from Data: Training, Loss, and Optimization
Before we can train a model to make good predictions, we need to understand how it
learns from mistakes. In this section, we’ll see how a network “watches game tape” of
its own predictions and corrects its play, using three key ideas:
Backpropagation
How errors flow backward through the network
Gradient descent
How the network uses those errors to adjust its weights step by step
Regularization
How the network balances fitting the data with maintaining generalization
Backpropagation
Backpropagation is the method a neural network uses to learn from errors. Imagine
replaying a failed attack in slow motion: the team builds the play from the back,
works the ball forward, creates a shot, and then fails to score because the attempt
is blocked, saved, or off target. The coach then rewinds the move from the shot all
the way back through the earlier passes, pointing out where the buildup could be
improved. In a similar fashion, backpropagation (also called backprop) takes the pre‐
diction error at the output layer and works backward through the network, showing
each weight how much it contributed to the mistake.
The key output of this process is the gradient. It’s a measure of how much the loss
changes from changing a specific weight. By collecting these gradients layer by layer,
the network knows how to tweak itself so the next play (prediction) is a little more
accurate. Importantly, the learning signal begins at the final outcome, but the adjust‐
ments are not limited to the final layer. Earlier layers also change, just as a failed attack
may lead a team to adjust its buildup, spacing, or passing choices, not only the final
shot.

Backpropagation tells each weight how it contributed to an error; gradient descent is
how the network uses that feedback to improve. The idea is simple: adjust the weights
little by little in the direction that makes the loss smaller.

not restart from scratch after each mistake. Instead, it makes repeated small adjust‐

        y_batch = y_shuffled[i : i + batch_size]
        # Compute gradients and update weights
        grad_w, grad_b = compute_gradients(
            X_batch, y_batch, weights, bias
        )
        weights -= learning_rate * grad_w
        bias -= learning_rate * grad_b
Each epoch follows the same pattern:
1. Shuffle the matches (to avoid overfitting to order).
1.
2. Break them into smaller mini-batches.
2.
3. Compute gradients for each mini-batch and update the weights.
3.
Note that compute_gradients is a stand-in for the backpropagation process. In
practice, you won’t code this from scratch. Libraries like PyTorch and TensorFlow
automatically calculate gradients for you (via loss.backward() in PyTorch, or Gra
dientTape in TensorFlow).
Backpropagation tells us how the model was wrong, and gradient descent uses that
information to make the next update. That steady improvement is what turns error
signals into better weights and biases over time.
